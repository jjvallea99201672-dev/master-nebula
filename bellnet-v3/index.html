Principle 56

A complete archive is not a complete observation.

Early quiet may be fewer sensors, not a quieter Earth. M6+ is reasonably complete from the early 1900s, M5 from roughly the 1960s, and M4.5 from the 1970s. Smaller magnitudes are not loaded in Century Mode to avoid bias.

If playback gets denser in the 1970s, that is mostly instruments getting better — not the Earth getting busier.

Warning hidden. Press “Show Instrument Bias Warning” to bring back Principle 56.

Why clusters sound like flurries

Omori’s law: a big strike is followed by a flurry of aftershocks that thins out over time. You will hear a hard toll, then a rush that decays.

Gutenberg–Richter: small events vastly outnumber large ones — roughly 10× more events for each magnitude step down. That steady ratio is why Century Mode stays at M6+: adding smaller events would swamp the piece and the bias.

Shuffle Test

Play the same events, same bells, in random order and compare it with the real sequence. If a “pattern” survives shuffling, it may be in your ear — not in the Earth.

Hearing a pattern is a hypothesis, not a finding. Compare gaps between events versus a random (Poisson) baseline.

Great earthquakes arriving in global bursts or on regular cycles is disputed, with no consensus. Treat it as something to test, not assume.

This does NOT predict earthquakes.

Timing has not been predicted from the catalog. A pattern heard = a hypothesis to test. Bellnet helps you listen to what already happened.

HOW IT WORKS

From seismometer to bell.

01

Record

A seismometer records ground velocity — motion far below human hearing.

02

Map

Map the catalogue values to a bell: magnitude sets pitch, depth sets sustain, longitude sets stereo pan. The bell is synthesised — not a sped-up recording.

03

Ring

Map the event to a bell oscillator. Magnitude, energy, and depth shape the voice.
